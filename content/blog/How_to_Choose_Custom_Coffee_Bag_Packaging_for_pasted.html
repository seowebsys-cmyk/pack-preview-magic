Start with the roast and freshness window

Freshly roasted coffee releases carbon dioxide as it rests. If you pack coffee soon after roasting, a one-way degassing valve lets that gas escape while limiting oxygen entering the bag.

The valve and the oxygen barrier do different jobs. The valve manages gas release; the barrier slows oxygen exposure that can dull the coffee’s aroma and flavour.

Choose a resealable zipper when customers will open and close the bag over several servings. A tin tie offers a simple way to fold and hold the top closed, but it does not provide the same resealing action as a zipper.

Set your packing workflow around the coffee’s degassing and sales schedule. There is no need to assume every roast should be packed at the same interval; consider when it is ready for your chosen package and when it needs to reach customers.

When comparing custom coffee bags with a valve, consider the full combination of valve, barrier, seal, and closure. A feature chosen for appearance alone cannot replace the freshness protection your roast needs.

Compare pouch shapes for filling and display

A stand-up pouch uses a bottom gusset to stand on a shelf. It can be a practical choice when you want compact storage and a front-facing display, especially for retail or online product photography.

The Doypack is a stand-up pouch described as suitable for solid and liquid products, with size and material choices. Its standing format makes it a useful style to assess when shelf presentation and an efficient footprint matter.

A flat-bottom bag has a broad, stable base and a box-like presentation. Check how its shape feeds through your filling and sealing equipment, because the bag needs to work on the production line as well as on the shelf.

A gusseted bag expands to hold its contents, but its filling, sealing, and shelf behaviour depend on the exact construction. Test a filled sample in your workflow rather than choosing by appearance alone.

Look at how filled pouches hold their shape and resist wrinkling or compression in the carton you plan to ship. Transport can affect both package protection and the way your branded coffee bags look when they arrive.

Size the bag to the amount you sell

Match bag capacity to the coffee’s net weight and density. The same weight of whole-bean and ground coffee can occupy different volumes, so choose size by testing the form of coffee you will actually pack.

Compare filled samples at every planned weight. Leave enough room for a clean closure and seal, but avoid excessive headspace that can make the package look underfilled.

Think about the sales format, too. A compact bag may suit a single purchase or online shipment, while a larger size can work well for customers who buy more coffee at once.

The Flat Bottom Pouch has a stable, space-efficient shape and is available in 250g, 500g, and 1000g sizes, with a choice of material type. Those listed capacities give roasters several starting points for comparing filled size and shelf presence.

For a fair size comparison, fill each sample with the correct coffee and net weight, then set the bags beside one another. Check the front panel, top closure, and amount of unused space before finalising the dimensions.

Judge materials by protection and disposal

Laminated packaging can combine layers that perform different jobs, such as providing a barrier and supporting reliable sealing. Judge the complete structure, because a single layer does not describe the finished bag’s protection.

A kraft paper appearance does not establish that the full package is recyclable or compostable. Check the complete package structure and whether customers have a suitable local disposal route for it.

Material choice involves a trade-off between barrier performance and disposal options. Foil or metallized laminates can provide strong protection, but are often not accepted in standard curbside recycling because their layers are difficult to separate. Mono-material plastic structures may be more compatible with some recycling streams, but local acceptance depends on the complete pouch, including its valve, zipper, and other components. A kraft or paper-like surface can still hide plastic or foil layers. Compostable films also need the right certification and a local facility that accepts them; industrial compostability does not mean a bag belongs in home compost. Ask for the complete material structure and check the relevant local route before making a disposal claim.

Compare material options against the protection your roast needs, the sealing method you use, and the handling the bag will face during filling, storage, and delivery. Ask for the material and barrier specifications so you can assess those details together.

Make the design clear at every bag size

Design coffee packaging with a clear visual hierarchy: make the brand and coffee name easy to spot, then give the roast, origin, and other distinguishing details space to stay legible. Your coffee tells a story, and the package should make the important parts easy to find.

Custom coffee bags with a logo can build a consistent brand presence, but the front panel still needs to identify the product clearly. Include the product name and net weight, plus useful coffee details such as roast date and origin.

Make room for the business and product information required in the markets where you sell. Depending on the applicable rules, that may include the responsible business, lot identification, date marking, and other product details; do not rely on origin or roast information as a substitute for identifying the product as coffee.

Check your artwork at the smallest and largest planned bag sizes. Text and information placement that read well on one format can become crowded or difficult to read on another.

Choose colour to support the brand and distinguish coffees, rather than assuming one shade appeals to every customer. Strong contrast keeps text readable, while a consistent design system helps customers recognise different roasts.

Treat color findings as a starting point, not a forecast of how your customers will buy; results depend on the people, packaging, and choice being tested. Judge your own design from a print proof on the intended material, viewed under the lighting where customers will see it. Check that text stays legible against the color and that differences between roasts remain clear in a quick shelf scan.

Test samples and order around your workflow

Run a physical sample through your actual filling and sealing process. Check that the bag feeds correctly, fills without trouble, closes cleanly, and seals reliably with your equipment.

Then test the filled sample for standing stability, storage fit, closure use, and resistance to compression or damage in your shipping setup. This practical check helps you catch fit and handling issues before committing to a larger order.

For the shipping check, pack samples in the carton in the same arrangement and orientation you plan to use. After handling, look beyond whether the bag stayed sealed: check for a crushed base, creased front panel, shifted label, or valve pressed against another bag. If the package arrives looking compromised, adjust the carton layout or pouch format and repeat the fit check before approving the order.

Review a print proof at the intended bag size before approving production. Check colour, text legibility, and alignment, including how the design sits across gussets and near the seal.

Ask your supplier about the material and barrier specifications, valve and closure options, printing method, minimum order quantity, and production timing. If you use digital printing or another custom printing method, make sure its artwork requirements fit your design and planned run.

Request written quotes on the same pouch size, material and barrier, valve and closure, print coverage, and artwork versions so the comparison is fair. Check whether the minimum order applies per design or across the whole order, and what setup, artwork, proof, or sample charges include. Confirm what starts the production lead time, whether delivery is included in that schedule, and whether repeat orders have different terms. Supplier minimums and schedules vary with the print method and order details, so use the supplier’s written quote and schedule rather than relying on a general estimate.

Build that lead time into your roast schedule, launch date, and stock plan so your custom coffee packaging arrives when you need it.

Frequently Asked Questions

Should whole-bean and ground coffee use different bag sizes?

Often, yes, because grind size changes how coffee settles and packs inside the pouch. Run a fill test with the actual grind specification you will sell, especially if you offer both whole bean and ground versions of the same coffee.

How much headspace should I leave above the coffee before sealing the bag?

Leave enough clear space for the sealing area specified for your bag and sealing equipment. Keep beans or grounds out of that area, since coffee caught in the seal can interfere with a clean closure.

Can I use a tin tie instead of a resealable zipper?

Yes, if the bag’s top can be folded neatly and the tin tie sits where customers can wrap it securely. A tin tie is useful for holding a folded top closed, while customers who want a closure they can repeatedly open and press shut may prefer a zipper.

How long does roasted coffee stay fresh in an unopened bag?

There is no single freshness window for every roast and package; the coffee, barrier, valve, seal quality, and storage conditions all affect it. Set your best-before guidance using shelf-life testing on your own roast and finished bag, and store sealed packages away from heat, light, and moisture.

Can I print a different origin on each coffee bag in one order?

A shared package design can reserve a clear area for changing origin or lot details. Ask whether the chosen print process supports variable information and how separate artwork versions affect production setup and quantities.

Can I use the same coffee bag design for decaf and regular coffee?

You can keep shared brand elements while making the coffee type unmistakable on the front panel. Give decaf a clear label or visual cue so customers can distinguish it quickly when choosing between bags.
